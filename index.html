AI: From Inception to Today

Type
Research synthesis, visual timeline
Covers
1950 to 2025, 12 milestones, 2 AI winters
Tools
PowerPoint, primary sources

A four-slide history of AI that answers one question: why did progress stall twice, then take off?

I traced 75 years of AI through its booms and its two “winters,” when funding collapsed after results fell short of promises. Each milestone names the people and organizations behind it, so the story is about decisions, not just dates.

The finding I wanted readers to leave with: the comeback came when three things arrived together. Better algorithms kept alive through the winters, huge labelled datasets from the internet, and cheap computing from GPUs and the cloud.

For a data analyst, the lesson is practical: a model is only as good as the data and compute behind it, and hype cycles punish anyone who promises more than the evidence supports.

Chef Buddy: building a teaching chatbot

Type
AI Lab build, how-to write-up
Platform
Mizou
Skills
Prompt design, guardrails, deployment

I designed and launched a cooking coach that teaches students to cook, rather than just handing them a recipe.

Chef Buddy tailors each recipe to the student’s experience, allergies and kitchen equipment, explains terms like “sauté” as it goes, and ends with one cooking skill or food-science idea. Here’s how I built it, step by step, so you can build your own.

How I built it

  1. Defined the job. I wrote the bot’s instructions around a goal, building students’ confidence, so every answer teaches something and checks understanding with a quick question.
  2. Asked before answering. The bot asks about experience, dietary needs and equipment before it suggests anything, so the recipe fits the person.
  3. Set guardrails. Seven rules cover safety: always ask about allergies first, include knife and stove reminders, suggest an adult for younger students, never recommend raw flour or undercooked meat, and stay on topic.
  4. Chose not to add a knowledge file. The base model already knows cooking well. A file only makes sense when the bot must stick to specific material, like class-approved recipes.
  5. Packaged it. I wrote a short description and designed a bright, simple thumbnail that stays readable at a small size.
  6. Published and shared. I published the bot and created a session link. Users just enter a name, no account needed, and I can review their conversations.
Chef Buddy’s opening messageHi, I’m Chef Buddy! Ready to cook something delicious? Tell me what you’re in the mood for, or list a few ingredients you have, and I’ll help you turn them into a meal. Do you have any food allergies I should know about first?

What I learned

Most of the work in a chatbot isn’t the AI, it’s the specification. Clear goals, the right questions up front and firm rules did more for quality than any setting. It’s the same discipline as scoping an analysis: decide what a good answer looks like before you start.

Rating myself, then rating the ratings

Type
Reflection, survey critique
Instruments
Change Management and AI/ML Integration Leadership self-assessments
Skills
Survey design, data quality, self-reflection

I took two leadership self-assessments, found my strengths and gaps, and then treated the forms themselves as data to be checked.

Both forms told the same story. I’m strongest at understanding: what AI can do, how change works and how people experience it. I’m weakest at execution: project management, spotting skills gaps and working through conflict.

Then I questioned the data

Looking at my own numbers as an analyst would, they didn’t hold up well. I rated over half the Change Management items a 5 and never went below a 3 on either form. Some answers contradicted each other: a 5 for guiding teams through tech transitions, but a 3 for preparing people for AI changes.

The forms had design problems that made inflation easy. Only the ends of the 1–5 scale were defined. Several items asked two things at once. Sections were scored out of 10, 15, 20 or 30, so totals couldn’t be compared.

What I’d change

Define every point on the scale with an observable behavior. Require one real example for any 5. Add a colleague’s rating beside my own. Split double-barreled items and standardize the scoring. For the change form, I’d add items from the Center for Creative Leadership’s research on successful change leaders, such as explaining why a change is needed and engaging resistant stakeholders instead of avoiding them.

A self-assessment is most useful when it is hard to rate yourself a 5.

Those changes would probably lower my scores. That’s the point, and it’s the habit I bring to any dataset: before trusting a number, ask how it was measured.
