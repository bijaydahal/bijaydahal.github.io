Artifact 1

AI: From Inception to Today

Type
Research synthesis, visual timeline
Covers
1950 to 2025, 12 milestones, 2 AI winters
Format
4-slide presentation
  • Introduction
  • Description
  • Value proposition
  • References

Introduction

A four-slide history of AI that answers one question: why did progress stall twice, then take off?

I traced 75 years of AI through its booms and its two “winters,” when funding collapsed after results fell short of promises. Each milestone names the people and organizations behind it, so the story is about decisions, not just dates.

Description

Objective

Explain the history of artificial intelligence in a way a busy reader can absorb in two minutes, and identify the conditions that turned decades of slow progress into today’s rapid adoption.

Process
  1. Gathered milestones. I collected candidate events from 1950 to 2025 using original papers and reports wherever possible, such as Turing’s 1950 paper and the 1955 Dartmouth proposal.
  2. Framed one question. Instead of listing everything, I asked why progress stalled twice and then accelerated, and kept only the 12 milestones that help answer it.
  3. Marked the winters. I placed the two funding collapses (mid-1970s and late 1980s to early 1990s) on the same strip as the booms, so the pattern is visible at a glance.
  4. Ended on causes. The final slide names the three drivers of the comeback: better algorithms, large labelled datasets and cheap computing from GPUs and the cloud.
Tools and tech used
  • Microsoft PowerPoint
  • Primary sources and peer-reviewed papers
  • Timeline and information design

Value proposition

Unique value

Most AI timelines are lists of dates. This one is built around a single question and ends with an answer, the three conditions that had to arrive together. It reads like an analysis, not a list.

Relevance

Leaders deciding whether to invest in AI need to know that hype cycles punish anyone who promises more than the evidence supports. For an analyst, the lesson is practical: a model is only as good as the data and compute behind it.

Chef Buddy: Building a Teaching Chatbot

Introduction

I designed and launched a cooking coach that teaches students to cook, rather than just handing them a recipe.

Chef Buddy tailors each recipe to the student’s experience, allergies and kitchen equipment, explains terms like “sauté” as it goes, and ends with one cooking skill or food-science idea. This write-up shows how I built it, step by step, so others can build their own.

Description

Objective

Build a safe, focused AI assistant that builds students’ cooking confidence. The hard part: students may be young, have allergies, or be working with knives and heat, and a general chatbot will happily answer anything.

Process
  1. Defined the job. I wrote the bot’s instructions around one goal, building students’ confidence, so every answer teaches something and checks understanding with a quick question.
  2. Asked before answering. The bot asks about experience, dietary needs and equipment before it suggests anything, so the recipe fits the person.
  3. Set guardrails. Seven firm rules cover safety: always ask about allergies first, include knife and stove reminders, suggest an adult for younger students, never recommend raw flour or undercooked meat, and stay on topic.
  4. Chose not to add a knowledge file. The base model already knows cooking well. A file only makes sense when the bot must stick to specific material, like class-approved recipes.
  5. Packaged it. I wrote a short description and designed a bright, simple thumbnail that stays readable at a small size.
  6. Published and shared. I published the bot and created a session link. Users just enter a name, no account needed, and I can review their conversations to improve it.
Chef Buddy’s opening messageHi, I’m Chef Buddy! Ready to cook something delicious? Tell me what you’re in the mood for, or list a few ingredients you have, and I’ll help you turn them into a meal. Do you have any food allergies I should know about first?

Value proposition

Unique value

Chef Buddy coaches instead of just answering. It asks before it advises, enforces safety rules it can’t skip, and ends every exchange with something learned. The write-up doubles as a reusable guide for building any focused assistant.

Relevance

Most of the work in a chatbot isn’t the AI, it’s the specification. Any team deploying an assistant for customers, students or staff faces the same choices: what the bot is for, what it must ask first, and what it must never do. It’s the same discipline as scoping an analysis.

Rating Myself, Then Rating the Ratings

Type
Reflection, survey critique
Instruments
Change Management and AI/ML Integration Leadership self-assessments
  • Introduction
  • Description
  • Value proposition
  • References

Introduction

I took two leadership self-assessments, found my strengths and gaps, and then treated the forms themselves as data to be checked.

Both forms told the same story. I’m strongest at understanding: what AI can do, how change works and how people experience it. I’m weakest at execution: project management, spotting skills gaps and working through conflict.

Description

Objective

Identify my strengths and growth areas as a future AI and change leader, then test whether the self-assessment results could actually be trusted, and propose a better instrument.

Process
  1. Completed both assessments honestly and converted each section score to a percentage so the two forms could be compared.
  2. Plotted the results to separate top strengths from growth areas.
  3. Audited my own answers for inflation and contradictions, the way I’d audit any dataset.
  4. Audited the forms against basic survey-design principles: scale anchors, double-barreled items and comparable scoring.
  5. Proposed a redesign that is harder to inflate.

What the audit found

My numbers didn’t hold up well. I rated over half the Change Management items a 5 and never went below a 3 on either form. Some answers contradicted each other: a 5 for guiding teams through tech transitions, but a 3 for preparing people for AI changes.

The forms made inflation easy. Only the ends of the 1–5 scale were defined. Several items asked two things at once. Sections were scored out of 10, 15, 20 or 30, so totals couldn’t be compared.

What I’d change

Define every point on the scale with an observable behavior. Require one real example for any 5. Add a colleague’s rating beside my own. Split double-barreled items and standardize the scoring. For the change form, add items drawn from research on successful change leaders, such as explaining why a change is needed and engaging resistant stakeholders instead of avoiding them.

A self-assessment is most useful when it is hard to rate yourself a 5.

Tools and tech used
  • Change Management self-assessment
  • AI/ML Integration Leadership self-assessment
  • Score normalization
  • Survey-design audit
  • Dot-plot visualization (HTML/CSS)

Value proposition

Unique value

Most reflections stop at “here are my scores.” This one goes a step further and questions whether the scores mean anything, then proposes a fix that would probably lower them. That honesty is the point.

Relevance

Organizations make hiring, training and AI-readiness decisions from surveys like these every day. A leader who can spot a flawed instrument avoids decisions built on flattering but unreliable numbers. It’s the habit I bring to any dataset: before trusting a number, ask how it was measured.

Artifact 4

Data Challenge Scenarios: Thinking Like an ML Practitioner

Type
Coached practice exercise, reflection
Format
3 scenarios with an AI coach that probes, not answers
  • Introduction
  • Description
  • Value proposition
  • References

Introduction

I worked through three data problems that machine-learning teams actually face, with an AI coach that kept asking “why?” and “what could still go wrong?”

There was no answer key. For each scenario I proposed an approach, defended it, and then dealt with whatever the coach pushed on: a hidden assumption, a tradeoff, or a risk my first answer missed.

Description

Objective

Practice the judgment ML teams need on real data problems (quality, bias and privacy) where several answers are defensible and naming a technique isn’t enough. I had to explain what each technique does and what it costs.

Process

For each scenario I committed to a plan, backed it with reasons and a way to test it, and revised when a question exposed a gap.

  1. Scenario 1 · Data quality
    Cleaning 800,000 customer reviews

    Duplicates, bot spam, many languages, emoji-only reviews, and sentiment labels taken from star ratings.

    My approach: remove exact and near-duplicates (using a similarity score) before splitting train and test data, so the model is never graded on reviews it has already seen. Then compare vocabulary and rating distributions before and after cleaning to check that no language or reviewer group quietly disappeared.

    What changed my mind: I first wanted to translate everything into one language and give every emoji a fixed number. A sarcastic 🙄 or a Nepali slang phrase carries meaning that this kind of standardizing erases. Unusual data isn’t bad data.

  2. Scenario 2 · Bias and scaling
    Taking a résumé screener to a new country at 10× volume

    Risks I flagged: foreign credentials and CV formats the model has never seen, recruiter bias and proxies baked into ten years of decisions, a target that predicts past hiring habits rather than job performance, and recruiters rubber-stamping rejections once volume grows tenfold.

    Evidence I’d require: a small benchmark labeled from blind résumés by local hiring managers, a staged human-led rollout, and error rates by subgroup. If only 1–3% of applicants are hired, a model that rejects everyone still scores about 97% accuracy.

  3. Scenario 3 · Privacy and governance
    Two hospitals, one diagnostic model, no raw data shared

    My design: federated learning on a shared data standard (OMOP or FHIR), differential privacy (DP-SGD) on the model updates, verified participants, encrypted traffic, a joint committee so neither hospital controls the model alone, and separate testing at each site.

    The tradeoff: limiting any single patient’s influence protects privacy, but it can make the model miss rare conditions, which means more false negatives for exactly the patients who need an accurate diagnosis.

What I learned

The three scenarios are one story. Messy labels weaken what a model learns, historical decisions can teach it patterns nobody should keep, a model that works in one place can fail in another, and privacy rules decide what data can be used at all. A single accuracy number can hide every one of these problems.

The coach raised risks I didn’t: checking whether star ratings are trustworthy labels, giving rejected applicants a way to appeal, monitoring after launch, and defending against poisoned updates from a trusted partner. That’s where I’m focusing next.

Every safeguard leaves some risk behind. The useful question is what’s left.

Tools and tech used
  • AI coaching chatbot
  • Near-duplicate detection
  • Subgroup error analysis
  • Federated learning
  • Differential privacy (DP-SGD)
  • OMOP / HL7 FHIR data standards

Value proposition

Unique value

This isn’t a list of correct answers. It shows reasoning under pressure: committing to a plan, defending it, and changing my mind on the record when a better argument came along, including the risks I missed.

Relevance

Any organization training or buying an ML model faces these exact problems: dirty data, biased history and privacy limits. Teams need people who ask how a result was measured and who it might be wrong about before it reaches customers, applicants or patients.
